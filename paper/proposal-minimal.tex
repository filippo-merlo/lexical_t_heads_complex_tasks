\title{Lexical Task Heads beyond Simple Tasks:\\ Logit Lens and Jacobian Lens Readouts}
\author{Filippo Merlo}
\date{Draft --- \today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\phantomsection\label{sec:abstract}
Lexical task heads are attention heads whose output, decoded into the vocabulary, names the task the prompt asks for \citep{yang2026shared}. They were identified with the per-head logit lens on simple single-step tasks, such as mapping a country to its capital. We ask whether the same method finds them for complex tasks defined by explicit rules \citep{fu2026rules}, which need several inference steps, and whether Head-JLens \citep{merlo2026headjlens}, which decodes each head's output after mapping it through a Jacobian of the remaining layers averaged over many texts, finds lexical task heads that the logit lens misses. In Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, we identify lexical task heads under both readouts on 14 simple tasks and 5 complex tasks at their easy level, with rule-based and example-based prompts, and test each head set by activation patching.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
A language model's accuracy on a task can change a great deal with the wording of the prompt that asks for it: on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, prompts for the same antonym items differ in accuracy by up to a factor of four \citep[Fig.~1]{yang2026shared}. \citet{yang2026shared} explain part of this variation with \emph{lexical task heads}, attention heads whose outputs, decoded into the vocabulary with the logit lens, name the task (``opposite'' for antonyms, ``cities'' for capitals). The heads are largely shared between instruction-based and example-based prompts, patching their mean activation into a prompt without task information raises task accuracy, and prompts that fail activate them less.

Two limits of that work motivate this project. First, its 17 tasks are simple single-step mappings (country to capital, word to antonym), and the authors note that ``it remains unclear if these shared lexical mechanisms hold for more abstract tasks or tasks that are less easily defined by simple vocabulary clusters'' \citep[Sec.~4]{yang2026shared}. \citet{fu2026rules} provide such tasks: five novel tasks defined by explicit rules (a card game, a board game, a composed arithmetic operator, noun-class agreement in an artificial language, and lexical-category inference), each at three difficulty levels and each given as rules, as examples or as both. Second, the logit lens decodes a head's output directly and leaves out the computation of the layers that follow it; \citet[Sec.~4]{yang2026shared} note that task representations ``encoded in a non-lexical, abstract format'' may not project cleanly to tokens. Head-JLens reads each head through a Jacobian map of the remaining layers, and on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct it ranks annotated intermediate words, which the prompt never states (such as the bridge entity of a two-hop question), higher than the per-head logit lens at most layers on five of six evaluation sets \citep[Sec.~3]{merlo2026headjlens}.

We therefore ask whether \citeauthor{yang2026shared}'s method identifies lexical task heads for the complex tasks of \citet{fu2026rules}, and how the logit lens and Head-JLens compare as readouts for identifying them.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A test of the lexical-task-head method on complex, rule-defined tasks, with a causal check of the identified heads.
\item A comparison of the per-head logit lens and Head-JLens for identifying lexical task heads, on simple and complex tasks.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:background}
\paragraph{Task representations across prompting styles.} Function vectors are sums of the mean outputs of a few attention heads that, added to the residual stream, trigger a task in another context \citep{todd2024function}. \citet{davidson2025different} extract them from instructions as well as from demonstrations and find that the two prompting styles engage mostly different heads: of the 20 top heads in each model, 2 to 7 are shared. \citet{yang2026shared} identify a different kind of task representation, lexical task heads, whose outputs decode to words describing the task; 73\% of them are shared between instruction-based and example-based prompts on average, and they are largely disjoint from function-vector heads \citep[App.~I]{yang2026shared}. We test the lexical-task-head method on complex tasks.

\paragraph{Reading attention heads in the vocabulary.} The logit lens decodes an intermediate state with the model's final normalization and unembedding \citep{nostalgebraist2020logitlens}; applied to one head's output, it is the readout of \citet{yang2026shared}. The Jacobian lens of \citet{gurnee2026workspace} instead maps a residual-stream state through a Jacobian of the remaining layers, averaged over many texts. Head-JLens moves this map to the concatenated head outputs, so that each head is read through the layers that follow it; in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct it ranks annotated intermediate words higher than the per-head logit lens at most layers on five of six evaluation sets, and heads selected with it are better sites for edits \citep{merlo2026headjlens}. We compare the two readouts on a different use: identifying task-descriptive heads.

\paragraph{Learning tasks from rules and from examples.} \citet{fu2026rules} compare learning from rules, from examples and from both on five novel tasks. Rules outperform examples overall, adding examples to rules gives no significant gain, and the advantage of rules is largest for tasks that need algebraic computation. Their study is behavioural; we use their tasks to study task representations.

\section{Research questions}\label{sec:rq}
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] Does the method of \citet{yang2026shared} identify lexical task heads for the complex tasks of \citet{fu2026rules} under rule-based and example-based prompts, and do the identified heads have a causal effect on task accuracy?
\item[RQ2] How do the per-head logit lens and Head-JLens compare as readouts for identifying lexical task heads, on simple and on complex tasks?
\item[RQ3] Are the lexical task heads of a complex task shared between rule-based and example-based prompts, as \citet{yang2026shared} find for simple tasks?
\end{description}

\begin{hypothesis}[replication]\label{h:rep}
On the simple tasks, Logit-head reproduces \citet{yang2026shared}: lexical task heads are found for every task, and patching them into prompts without task information raises accuracy above the unpatched baseline. \emph{Falsified} if, on the same model, the method finds no heads or no accuracy gain for tasks where \citet{yang2026shared} report both.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{hypothesis}[complex tasks]\label{h:complex}
Following the limitation stated by \citet[Sec.~4]{yang2026shared}, the complex tasks yield fewer lexical task heads per task and a smaller causal effect than the simple tasks under Logit-head. \emph{Falsified} if they yield as many heads with as large an effect.
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{hypothesis}[readouts]\label{h:readout}
Following \citet{merlo2026headjlens}, J-head identifies at least the lexical task heads that Logit-head identifies, and the heads found only by J-head have a causal effect above baseline. \emph{Falsified} if the J-head set misses heads of the Logit-head set, or if the heads found only by J-head have no causal effect.
\end{hypothesis}

\paragraph{Scope.} We do not explain behavioural variation across wordings (the failure analyses of \citealp[Sec.~2.4]{yang2026shared}), and we use one model.

\section{Framework}\label{sec:framework}
\paragraph{Tasks and prompts.} A task $t$ is given by a dataset $D_t$ of inputs and correct outputs. For the complex tasks we use the easy level of each (Sec.~\ref{sec:tasks}). Each prompt for $t$ has a style $s\in\{\text{rule},\text{example},\text{combined}\}$: its task information is a rule text, a set of demonstrations, or both, followed by the input $x$. $P_{t,s}$ is the set of prompts of style $s$ for $t$, and $P^{+}_{t,s}\subseteq P_{t,s}$ those the model answers correctly and straight away, with the answer as the first generated text (in reasoning mode, Sec.~\ref{sec:reasoning}: correctly after reasoning).

\paragraph{Heads and readouts.} Following \citet[Sec.~2]{merlo2026headjlens}, $z_{l,h}(p)$ is the output of head $h$ in layer $l$ at the last position of $p$, before the output projection, and $c_{l,h}(p)=W^{(h)}_{O,l}z_{l,h}(p)$ its write to the residual stream. With $q_v=g\odot w_v$ the gain-scaled unembedding row of token $v$, the two readouts score $v$ by
\[
\ell^{\mathrm{L}}_{l,h}(p)[v]=q_v^\top c_{l,h}(p)\quad\text{(Logit-head)},\qquad
\ell^{\mathrm{J}}_{l,h}(p)[v]=q_v^\top J_{l,h}\,z_{l,h}(p)\quad\text{(J-head)}.
\]
$T^{R}_{k,l,h}(p)$ is the set of the $k$ highest-scoring tokens under readout $R\in\{\mathrm{L},\mathrm{J}\}$, and $V_t$ the term list of task $t$.

\begin{definition}[Lexical task head]\label{def:lth}
Following \citet[Sec.~2.2.2]{yang2026shared}, head $(l,h)$ is a lexical task head for $t$ on prompt $p$ under readout $R$ if $|T^{R}_{k,l,h}(p)\cap V_t|\geq n$. It is a lexical task head for $t$ and style $s$ if this holds for at least a fraction $\rho$ of the prompts in $P^{+}_{t,s}$. We use $n=1$, $k=10$, $\rho=0.1$, as \citet{yang2026shared}. $H^{R}_{t,s}$ denotes the set of such heads.
\end{definition}

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Model}\label{sec:model}
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct: it is the main model of \citet{yang2026shared}, and the only model with released Head-JLens matrices.

\subsection{Tasks}\label{sec:tasks}
\paragraph{Simple tasks.} The 14 tasks used by both \citet{yang2026shared} and \citet{davidson2025different}, with the datasets of \citet{todd2024function}: antonym, synonym, singular--plural, present--past, country--capital, country--currency, landmark--country, park--country, product--company, next-item, prev-item, English--French, English--German, English--Spanish.
Rule-based prompts: the five instruction templates per task of \citet[App.~A.3.1]{yang2026shared}.
Example-based prompts: 1, 5, 10, 20 and 30 shots \citep[App.~A.3]{yang2026shared}.

\paragraph{Complex tasks.} The five tasks of \citet{fu2026rules} at their easy level: Set Game, Tapatan, Operator Function, Noun Class Agreement, Lexical Category Inference. Data from their public release: 300 test items per task, drawn with their sampler and seed (all 600 for Lexical Category Inference), the same items under every prompt.
Rule-based prompts: their rules-only prompts \citep[App.~F]{fu2026rules} and further wordings (Sec.~\ref{sec:trmethods}).
Example-based and combined prompts: their minimum-coverage demonstration sets \citep[Sec.~2.5]{fu2026rules}.

\paragraph{Why these tasks.} Lexical task heads and their term lists were defined on the simple tasks \citep{yang2026shared}, so the method applies unchanged and our results can be compared with theirs. \citet[Sec.~4]{yang2026shared} restrict their study to tasks with simple lexical descriptions. The complex tasks are novel, need several inference steps, and are given in rule, example and combined forms that specify the same task on the same test items, so the task is held fixed while the way it is communicated varies \citep[Sec.~1--2]{fu2026rules}.

\paragraph{Answer format.} As in the source studies: the templates of \citet{yang2026shared} for the simple tasks; the answer formats and generation limits of \citet[App.~A]{fu2026rules} for the complex tasks, with greedy decoding (\citeauthor{fu2026rules} sample at temperature 0.1), so that the prompt whose heads are read and the scored answer always match. This keeps accuracy comparable with the reported results. \citeauthor{fu2026rules}'s instruction-tuned models often explain before answering, so forcing a direct answer would be a different setting. Lexical task heads are read at the last prompt token, where the answer is produced only if the model answers straight away; prompts on which it explains first are therefore left out of $P^{+}_{t,s}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:framework}), except for the tasks run in reasoning mode (Sec.~\ref{sec:reasoning}).

\subsection{Task-representation methods}\label{sec:trmethods}
\paragraph{Lexical task heads} \citep[Sec.~2.2.2]{yang2026shared}.
Each attention head's output at the last prompt token is decoded with the two readouts of Sec.~\ref{sec:framework}: Logit-head, the per-head logit lens used by \citet{yang2026shared} \citep{nostalgebraist2020logitlens}, and J-head (Head-JLens), which applies the head's columns $J_{l,h}$ of the per-layer Jacobian map fitted on WikiText-103 \citep[Sec.~2]{merlo2026headjlens}. Fitted matrices for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct are released with \citet[Sec.~S4]{merlo2026headjlens}.
Under each readout, lexical task heads are identified with Definition~\ref{def:lth}.
For the simple tasks, $P^{+}_{t,s}$ is taken as in the code of \citet{yang2026shared}: the first 100 prompts answered correctly under one wording, the 5-shot prompts for the example style and the instruction template with the highest accuracy for the rule style. The complex tasks follow the same rule: the first 100 such prompts under the most accurate rule wording, under the example-based prompts and under the combined prompts.
Term lists: \citet[App.~A.2.1]{yang2026shared} for the simple tasks; for the complex tasks, see below.

\paragraph{Simple tasks as in the source code.} The simple tasks are run as in the code of \citet{yang2026shared}, with three departures. First, padding: their code passes prompts to the model in batches of 20, padded on the left, without an attention mask, so that the model attends to the padding; we pass the mask and the matching positions, so that each prompt is computed as if run alone. Second, the J-head readout. Third, the precision of the forward pass: bfloat16, in which the Head-JLens matrices were fitted.

\paragraph{What we add to the complex tasks.}\label{sec:adapt} \citet{fu2026rules} provide the data, one rules-only prompt per task and difficulty level, and the example-based and combined prompts. To apply lexical task heads to them we add three things.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Rule wordings}: six per original rule prompt (App.~\ref{app:orig}), each stating exactly the same rules. They are generated with GPT-5.5 through the Azure OpenAI API from a paraphrase prompt that keeps placeholders, output labels and the answer-format instruction verbatim (App.~\ref{app:genprompts}), and each is checked by us against the original; App.~\ref{app:wordings} lists them. \citet{yang2026shared} use five instruction templates per simple task; the complex tasks have six rule wordings besides the original, and their rule-style $P^{+}$ is taken from the most accurate of the seven.
\item \emph{Term lists} of task-descriptive words, of the kind in \citet[App.~A.2.1]{yang2026shared}: single words that describe the task rather than its answers, with case and number variants. They are generated with GPT-5.5 through the Azure OpenAI API from the rule prompt (App.~\ref{app:genprompts}), as \citet[App.~O]{yang2026shared} generated term lists with LLMs, and checked by us. Each word is expanded to token ids as in \citet{yang2026shared}: as written, with a leading space, in upper, capitalised and lower case, and after a \texttt{.}, \texttt{\_} or \texttt{-}, keeping every variant that is a single token of the model's tokenizer. There is one list per original rule prompt (App.~\ref{app:orig}): Tapatan's levels share one prompt and change only board size, piece count and line length, whereas the other tasks add rules or, for Operator Function, change the function \citep[Table~1, App.~F]{fu2026rules}. Lists are task-specific, not item-specific: for Lexical Category Inference, whose category definitions change from item to item, the list contains only words that describe the task itself. App.~\ref{app:terms} lists them.
\item \emph{Prompts without task information} for the causal check (Sec.~\ref{sec:causal}): the input block of the rules-only prompt alone (the lines that introduce the input and the answer, such as \texttt{Sentence: \{sentence\}} and \texttt{Answer:}), without rules or examples, the counterpart of the zero-shot prompts of \citet[Sec.~2.3.2]{yang2026shared}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Reasoning mode.}\label{sec:reasoning} For the complex tasks on which E0 finds that the model does not answer straight away (Set Game, Operator Function and Tapatan), we follow the instruction-tuned setup of \citet[App.~A]{fu2026rules} and let the model reason: chat template, greedy decoding, and an output limit of 6,000 new tokens, their limit for Operator Function, for all of these tasks. Answers are extracted as in their released code: after whitespace is collapsed, the first \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, read as three cards of the form \texttt{(animal | biome | \ldots)} for Set Game and as an integer for Operator Function. Their code has no parser for Tapatan; we take the first of \texttt{A win}, \texttt{B win}, \texttt{continue} in the output. An output without an extractable answer within the limit is wrong. Generation stops once this answer is complete, since nothing after it can change the score. $P^{+}_{t,s}$ then contains the prompts whose final answer is correct, whatever precedes it. Definition~\ref{def:lth} is applied at two positions: the last prompt token, as for the other tasks, where heads show the task as identified from the prompt; and the pre-answer position, the token after which the first answer token is written, found by one forward pass over the prompt and the model's own output, where heads show the task as represented when the answer is produced. At the pre-answer position, the copy share also checks the generated text. Heads at the last prompt token are compared across all tasks; heads at the pre-answer position and the accuracies after reasoning are reported separately. These tasks use the same 300 items as the others; where items share a prompt (Tapatan's sample repeats 46 boards), the prompt is answered once and its reply scored for each of them.

\paragraph{Why these readouts.} Lexical task heads locate a task representation and give its content in words \citep{yang2026shared}. Logit-head reproduces their readout. J-head reads each head through the layers that follow it: in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct it ranks unstated intermediate words higher than Logit-head at most layers on five of six evaluation sets, and its readouts are better guides for edits \citep[Secs.~3--4]{merlo2026headjlens}. A lexical task head's decoded task word is likewise not the answer, and \citet[Sec.~4]{yang2026shared} note that their logit-lens criterion may miss task representations that do not project cleanly to tokens.

\subsection{Causal check}\label{sec:causal}
The causal check of \citet[Sec.~2.3.2, App.~G]{yang2026shared} tests whether a head set carries the task: the heads' mean output on task prompts is written into prompts that do not state the task, and we measure whether the model then performs it.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Patched prompts}: prompts without task information, the zero-shot prompts of \citet{yang2026shared} for the simple tasks and the input blocks of Sec.~\ref{sec:adapt} for the complex tasks, for the first 100 items answered correctly with one shot (simple tasks) or under the original rules prompt (complex tasks). Their unpatched accuracy is the baseline.
\item \emph{Mean output}: for each head in the set, the mean of $c_{l,h}$ over the first 100 prompts of a wording answered correctly, leaving out the patched items. As in the code of \citet{yang2026shared}, each wording of a style (each instruction template, each number of shots, each rule wording) gives its own mean and its own patch. For the simple tasks, the head set of each style is patched with the means of both styles. For the complex tasks, only the rule-style head sets are patched, with the means of the seven rule wordings.
\item \emph{Patch}: at the last position of each patched prompt, the output of every head in the set is replaced by its mean output times a scaling factor $\alpha\in\{-10,-6,-4,-2,0,1,2,\dots,10\}$, the values of \citet{yang2026shared}. The model answers from the patched prompt, and the answer is scored as in E0.
\item \emph{Control}: the same patch at the same heads, with the mean output of a different task. For the simple tasks, the control pairs of \citet{yang2026shared}, with the mean taken as in their code: over the control task's 1-shot prompts, at all the items it answers correctly with one shot (for example-style means) or under the second instruction template (for rule-style means). For the complex tasks, a task with the same answer format (Noun Class Agreement and Lexical Category Inference with each other, Set Game and Operator Function with each other, Tapatan with Set Game), with the mean taken under its original rules prompt. A head set carries the task if its own mean raises accuracy and the control mean does not; a gain under both points to the answer format rather than the task.
\end{enumerate}
Three head sets are patched for each task and style: $H^{\mathrm{L}}_{t,s}$, $H^{\mathrm{J}}_{t,s}$, and the heads found only by J-head, $H^{\mathrm{J}}_{t,s}\setminus H^{\mathrm{L}}_{t,s}$. We report the accuracy at each $\alpha$ averaged over the wordings of the mean's style: five for the simple tasks, the seven rule wordings for the complex tasks.
In reasoning mode (Sec.~\ref{sec:reasoning}), each set's mean output is taken at the position where the set was found. It is patched at the last prompt position and kept at every generated position, and the final answer is scored. To bound the cost of long generations, 10 items are patched, with $\alpha\in\{0,1,4,10\}$.

\paragraph{Second check.} The code of \citet{yang2026shared} releases one script for this check (\texttt{causal\_fix\_incorrect.py}), and it differs from the procedure described in their App.~G.1, which we follow above, in the patched prompts. Instead of the zero-shot prompts, it patches the 1-shot prompts of the first 100 items answered wrongly with one shot, or all of them where fewer are wrong; these prompts show the answer format, and their unpatched accuracy is 0 by construction. Head sets, means, $\alpha$ and scoring are as above, and the script has no control. We run it as a second check and report both. For the complex tasks, the patched prompts are the original rules prompts of the first 100 items answered wrongly under them (10 in reasoning mode). On a task with two labels, a patch that only moves the model's answers towards one label already raises accuracy on these items, to the share of that label among them; we compare the gains with this share.

\subsection{Measures}\label{sec:measures}
For each task $t$, style $s$ and readout $R$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Accuracy} of each prompt, with the evaluation of the source paper: that of \citet{yang2026shared} for the simple tasks and that of \citet{fu2026rules} for the complex tasks.
\item \emph{Head count} $|H^{R}_{t,s}|$ and the distribution of the heads over layers.
\item \emph{Overlap} between two head sets $A$ and $B$: $|A\cap B|/|A|$ and $|A\cap B|/|B|$. Compared pairs: $H^{\mathrm{L}}_{t,s}$ and $H^{\mathrm{J}}_{t,s}$ (RQ2); $H^{R}_{t,\text{rule}}$ and $H^{R}_{t,\text{example}}$ (RQ3).
\item \emph{Causal effect}: the highest accuracy over $\alpha$ minus the unpatched accuracy, for the head set and for the control.
\item \emph{Copy share}: the share of prompts on which a head's matched terms ($T^{R}_{k,l,h}(p)\cap V_t$) occur in the prompt text, averaged over the heads of $H^{R}_{t,s}$. It separates heads that may copy task words from the prompt from heads that do not.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
\subsection{E0: Behavioural baseline}\label{sec:e0}
\emph{Question}: on which tasks does the model perform above chance? \emph{Procedure}: accuracy of every wording of every style, on all tasks, and the share of prompts on which the model does not answer straight away. \emph{Output}: the pairs of task and style used in E1 and E2, those whose accuracy exceeds chance, and the prompts $P^{+}_{t,s}$. The share of prompts left out decides whether other ways of handling them are needed.

\subsection{E1: Simple tasks}\label{sec:e1}
\emph{Question}: H\ref{h:rep}, and RQ2 on the simple tasks. \emph{Procedure}: identify $H^{\mathrm{L}}_{t,s}$ and $H^{\mathrm{J}}_{t,s}$ for the 14 simple tasks, for rule-based and example-based prompts (heads from the most accurate of Yang's five instruction templates and from 5-shot prompts; means from all five templates and from 1, 5, 10, 20 and 30 shots); compute the measures of Sec.~\ref{sec:measures}; run the causal check (Sec.~\ref{sec:causal}). \emph{Decides}: whether Logit-head reproduces \citet{yang2026shared} on our setup, and how J-head differs from it where the method is known to work.

\subsection{E2: Complex tasks}\label{sec:e2}
\emph{Question}: RQ1, H\ref{h:complex}, RQ2 on the complex tasks, and RQ3. \emph{Procedure}: as in E1, for each complex task kept by E0, with the added wordings and term lists (Sec.~\ref{sec:adapt}); example-based and combined prompts from \citet{fu2026rules}. \emph{Decides}: whether lexical task heads are found and causally effective for complex tasks, under which readout, and whether they are shared with example-based prompts.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
Each experiment was run once, with greedy decoding. Tables~\ref{tab:e0-simple}--\ref{tab:effects-complex-wrong} and Figures~\ref{fig:layers}--\ref{fig:patch-complex} are generated from the result files by \texttt{scripts/results\_tex.py}. Causal-check accuracies are over the patched items: 100 per task, and 10 in reasoning mode, where one item is 0.1. Still running: the causal checks of Operator Function and Tapatan, and Tapatan's rule wordings 5 and 6. Tapatan's rule-style heads are therefore taken from wording~1, the most accurate of the original prompt and wordings 1 to 4, and may change.

\subsection{E0: Behavioural baseline}\label{sec:res-e0}
\paragraph{Simple tasks} (Table~\ref{tab:e0-simple}). Every task is answered well above its zero-shot accuracy under both styles, and the zero-shot prompts, which are the prompts without task information of the causal check, are almost never answered correctly. Accuracy rises from one to five shots and changes little after that. The instruction templates differ most on the translation tasks, next-item, prev-item, park--country and synonym, where one or two templates fall well below the others.

\paragraph{Complex tasks} (Table~\ref{tab:e0-complex}). Noun Class Agreement and Lexical Category Inference are answered straight away and above chance under all seven rule wordings and under examples. Combined prompts fall below chance, largely because the model often explains before answering: it answers straight away on 65\% (Noun Class Agreement) and 17\% (Lexical Category Inference) of combined prompts, against at least 96\% under every rule and example prompt. Without task information it never answers straight away.

Set Game, Operator Function and Tapatan are not answered straight away on any of their 300 items under any prompt, so they are run in reasoning mode (Sec.~\ref{sec:reasoning}); Table~\ref{tab:e0-complex} gives their accuracy after reasoning. Set Game and Operator Function are then solved under rules and combined prompts, and from examples alone on one item of 300 each, so their example-style $P^{+}$ is a single prompt. Operator Function varies most across wordings: every wrong answer under its rule wordings is a reply without a \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} answer, which the scoring counts as wrong, and under wording~2 only 3 of 300 replies have one. Tapatan stays close to chance under the rule wordings run so far and falls below it with examples and combined prompts, so its $P^{+}$ sets likely include items answered correctly by chance. Set Game is solved on 96 of 300 items without task information: its input block still asks which three cards on the board form a set.

\subsection{E1: Simple tasks}\label{sec:res-e1}
\paragraph{Head sets} (Table~\ref{tab:heads-simple}, Figures~\ref{fig:layers} and~\ref{fig:copy}). Logit-head finds lexical task heads for every task under both styles, from two heads (synonym) upward. J-head finds more heads than Logit-head on 23 of the 28 pairs of task and style. Two thirds of the heads it adds lie in layers 10 to 19, whereas most Logit-head heads lie in layer 20 or later (Figure~\ref{fig:layers}). The J-head set contains the whole Logit-head set on 5 of the 28 pairs, and at least half of it on every pair. Rule-style sets are at least as large as example-style sets on every task, and most heads of an example-style set are also in the rule-style set, except on synonym under both readouts and singular--plural under Logit-head. The copy share is high under most rule-style prompts, whose instructions contain task words, and lower on country--capital, singular--plural and synonym, where the heads also decode task words that the instruction does not contain; under example-style prompts it is close to zero except on country--capital (Figure~\ref{fig:copy}).

\paragraph{Causal check} (Tables~\ref{tab:effects-simple} and~\ref{tab:effects-simple-cross}, Figure~\ref{fig:patch-simple}). Patching the mean output of the Logit-head set, with means of its own style, raises accuracy above the unpatched baseline on country--capital, landmark--country, park--country and product--company under both styles, on country--currency, next-item and singular--plural with examples, and on prev-item with rules. It has no effect on antonym, synonym, present--past and the three translation tasks. Gains appear at positive $\alpha$. The control mean raises accuracy much less than the task mean, except on park--country, next-item and prev-item, where it raises it by a comparable amount or more.

The J-head set raises accuracy about as much as the Logit-head set or more on most tasks, and also on synonym, where the Logit-head set does not; landmark--country with examples is the clearest case where it raises it less. The heads found only by J-head raise accuracy by themselves on most tasks where the J-head set does, most strongly on singular--plural and country--capital with examples. The control mean at those heads raises it less, except on next-item and prev-item, where it raises it as much or more.

Patched with the means of the other style, the head sets raise accuracy on the same tasks, often by a comparable amount or more. On antonym and singular--plural, the rule-style sets raise accuracy only with example-style means: on antonym the Logit-head set, on singular--plural the J-head set and the heads only it finds. Present--past and the translation tasks show no gain under any pairing.

\paragraph{Second causal check} (Tables~\ref{tab:effects-simple-wrong} and~\ref{tab:effects-simple-wrong-cross}, Figure~\ref{fig:patch-simple-wrong}). With the wrongly answered 1-shot prompts patched, every head set raises accuracy above zero on every task, under both styles and with means of either style, though only by a few items on synonym and the translation tasks. This includes present--past and the translation tasks, where patching the zero-shot prompts has no effect, and antonym, where it has an effect only across styles. The gains are largest on singular--plural, country--capital and present--past and smallest on synonym and the translation tasks, the order reported by \citet[Figs.~57 and~62]{yang2026shared}. The curves also share features of theirs that the zero-shot prompts do not show, such as a gain at negative $\alpha$ on park--country. On landmark--country, park--country and product--company the gains are smaller than with the zero-shot prompts and closer to the reported ones. The J-head set raises accuracy about as much as the Logit-head set or more on most tasks, and far more on next-item and prev-item with examples. The heads found only by J-head raise it on every task where that set is not empty, least on landmark--country with examples.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.} For H\ref{h:rep}, lexical task heads are found for every simple task. With the zero-shot prompts described by \citet[App.~G.1]{yang2026shared}, patching them raises accuracy on eight tasks with means of the heads' own style, and on antonym with means of the other style; there is no gain on synonym under Logit-head, on present--past or on the three translation tasks. On three of the eight, park--country, next-item and prev-item, the control mean raises accuracy as well. With the 1-shot prompts of their released script, patching raises accuracy on all fourteen tasks, in the per-task pattern of their Figs.~57 and~62. H\ref{h:rep} is therefore falsified for the procedure their paper describes and holds for the setting of their released code, which reproduces their figures: the gains they report on present--past and the translation tasks need prompts that show the answer format. For H\ref{h:readout} on the simple tasks, the first condition fails, since the J-head set misses part of the Logit-head set on 23 of 28 pairs, and the second holds in both checks: heads found only by J-head have causal effects of their own.

\subsection{E2: Complex tasks}\label{sec:res-e2}
\paragraph{Head sets} (Table~\ref{tab:heads-complex}). Both readouts find lexical task heads for every complex task and style with a non-empty $P^{+}$, at the last prompt token and at the pre-answer position. The sets are larger than for the simple tasks, against H\ref{h:complex}, but the head counts of the two families are not comparable. The complex term lists expand to 114 to 340 token ids and the simple ones to 2 to 22, and a longer list makes a match among a head's ten top tokens more likely for any head. Small $P^{+}$ sets add to this: for the example style of Set Game and Operator Function, whose $P^{+}$ is one prompt, every head that matches on it is a lexical task head (9 to 21 heads per set), and for Tapatan's example and combined styles 9 of 89 and 5 of 47 matching prompts suffice. As on the simple tasks, J-head finds more heads than Logit-head on every pair, and three fifths of its added heads lie in layers 10 to 19. Its set covers 44\% to 91\% of the Logit-head set, never all of it. Of each example-style set, 43\% to 93\% is also in the rule-style set, more than half on all but two of the 16 pairs of task, position and readout (RQ3), as on most simple tasks; here the example-style sets of Set Game and Operator Function rest on one prompt. At the pre-answer position Tapatan has more heads than at the last prompt token, and Set Game and Operator Function similar numbers under rule and combined prompts. Copy shares lie between about half and nine tenths under rule and combined prompts and are lower under example prompts: zero on Set Game and Operator Function, and well above the near-zero values of the simple tasks' example prompts on the other three tasks (Figure~\ref{fig:copy}).

\paragraph{Causal check} (Tables~\ref{tab:effects-complex} and~\ref{tab:effects-complex-wrong}, Figure~\ref{fig:patch-complex}). The rule-style head sets are patched with the means of the seven rule wordings; in reasoning mode, only the checks of Set Game have finished. On Noun Class Agreement and Lexical Category Inference, the prompts without task information get no patched item right, since the model gives no answer straight away. On Noun Class Agreement, no patch changes this, for any head set, mean or $\alpha$. On Lexical Category Inference, the Logit-head and J-head sets change nothing with their own means. The heads found only by J-head, with the mean of wording~4, make the model answer ``No'' at $\alpha$ from 5 to 7, to 89 of the 100 items at $\alpha=6$, which reaches 0.56 (0.08 averaged over the seven wordings); the control mean at the J-head set and at the heads found only by J-head reaches 0.61, the share of ``No'' among the patched items (the constant-answer line in Figure~\ref{fig:patch-complex}). As in the earlier run with pooled means, the only gains are a constant ``No'', which the control produces as well: they fit a patch that makes the model give an answer of the required form, not one that supplies the task.

In the second check, the rules prompts answered wrongly, every head set raises accuracy above zero on both tasks, but only by moving answers towards one label. Patched at $\alpha=1$, with the heads' mean itself, the sets turn at most 21 of the 100 answers into correct ones under any one wording's mean, and at most 12 averaged over the seven. The largest gains come at $\alpha$ far from 1, where the model gives the same answer to (almost) every item. On Noun Class Agreement, every set answers ``No'' at $\alpha=-10$ (0.42, the share of ``No'' among these items), and the Logit-head set answers ``Yes'' at $\alpha=9$ (0.58, the share of ``Yes''), with each of the seven means. On Lexical Category Inference, setting the Logit-head set to zero ($\alpha=0$) makes the model answer ``Yes'' to most items (0.59, against 0.73 for answering ``Yes'' to all); $\alpha$ from 3 to 6 makes every set answer ``No'' (0.27); and the heads found only by J-head, with the means of the original prompt and wordings 2 and 4, answer ``Yes'' to almost every item at $\alpha$ of 7 or 8 (0.69 to 0.73). On these two-label tasks, the second check does not separate task information from a shift towards one answer.

On Set Game in reasoning mode, 3 of the 10 patched items are solved without task information. At the last prompt token, no patch raises accuracy above this: the Logit-head set with its own means reaches at most 4 items with one wording's mean (0.21 averaged over the seven), every other patch stays at or below 3, and in the second check no patch solves more than one item. At the pre-answer position, the three head sets patched at $\alpha=1$ with their own means solve about 6 of the 10 items (0.56 to 0.60 averaged over the seven means, and at least 4 with each mean), whereas the control means at the same heads and $\alpha$ solve 4 (Logit-head set), 2 (heads found only by J-head) and none (J-head set). Most of the items solved are ones the unpatched prompt gets wrong, and the patch loses some that it gets right: over the 21 conditions (three sets, seven means), the three items solved unpatched stay solved in 26 of 63 cases. Larger $\alpha$ lowers accuracy, to 0 at $\alpha=10$. In the second check at the pre-answer position, the patches reach at most 0.34 averaged over the seven means (the Logit-head set at $\alpha=1$), but setting the same heads to zero ($\alpha=0$) already solves 3 of the 10 wrongly answered items: on items answered after long reasoning, any change to the heads can change the outcome. The pre-answer gain in the first check is the only one on a complex task that the control mean does not match; it rests on 10 items.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.} For RQ1, the method finds lexical task heads for every complex task under both readouts, but on the two direct-answer tasks neither check shows a task-specific effect for them, nor on Set Game at the last prompt token. At Set Game's pre-answer position, the heads' own means raise accuracy above the unpatched prompt and above the control mean, on 10 items; the checks of Operator Function and Tapatan are still running. This largely agrees with the causal part of H\ref{h:complex}; its head-count part cannot be tested with term lists of such different lengths. For H\ref{h:readout} on the complex tasks, the first condition fails, since the J-head set misses part of the Logit-head set on every pair, and so does the second on the direct-answer tasks and at Set Game's last prompt token, where the heads found only by J-head have no task-specific effect; at Set Game's pre-answer position they have one, on 10 items. Together with E1, H\ref{h:readout} is falsified as stated, although on the simple tasks the heads that J-head adds have causal effects of their own.

\section{Plan}\label{sec:plan}
\paragraph{Steps.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Build the additions of Sec.~\ref{sec:adapt}: rule wordings (each checked to state the same rules as the original prompt), term lists, prompts without task information.
\item Run E0 and select tasks.
\item Run E1.
\item Run E2.
\end{enumerate}
Reused code and data: the lexical-task-head code of \citet{yang2026shared}; the Head-JLens code and matrices of \citet{merlo2026headjlens}; the data and generation code of \citet{fu2026rules}; the task datasets of \citet{todd2024function}.

\paragraph{Limitations.} One model, and one difficulty level of each complex task. The term lists are generated by an LLM and checked by hand, and the identified heads depend on them. Rule-based prompts contain task words, so a head may decode them by copying from the prompt; the copy share measures this but does not decide it. Under J-head, some sites rank tokens highly on unrelated prompts as well \citep[Sec.~3.6]{merlo2026headjlens}, so a larger J-head set is not by itself evidence of more task representation; the causal check is needed. Prompts on which the model explains before answering are left out, which may remove some tasks or styles from E1 and E2.

\paragraph{Deliverables.} Head sets, measures and causal-check curves for every task, style and readout.
